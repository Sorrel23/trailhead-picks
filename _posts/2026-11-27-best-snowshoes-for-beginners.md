Snowshoes turn a summer trail into a winter one. Once the snow gets deep, hiking boots sink to the knee with every step, and a walk that took an hour in July becomes a slog. A pair of snowshoes spreads your weight so you float on top instead. If you can walk, you can snowshoe.

The hard part is choosing. Beginner snowshoes range from foam "slippers for the snow" (as GearJunkie puts it) to aluminum-framed shoes with climbing bars, and the wrong pair can make a first outing miserable. We built this list from GearJunkie's and Outdoor Life's snowshoe testing, plus owner reviews, and focused on what matters most for new snowshoers: bindings that are easy with cold hands, enough grip for rolling trails, and the right size for your weight.

## How to choose beginner snowshoes

Size them by load, not shoe size. GearJunkie suggests checking each model's maximum recommended load: for day trips, it should be about 20 pounds more than your body weight to cover your pack. Longer snowshoes float better in powder, while shorter ones are easier to turn on packed trails and hills.

Think about where you'll go. If it's meadows, golf courses and groomed trails, flotation and an easy binding matter more than aggressive teeth. If the route climbs, look for a heel lift (a flip-up bar that saves your calves on steep grades) and more traction.

### 1. MSR Evo Trail — best overall for beginners

**Why it works:** GearJunkie calls it a go-to choice for beginners and casual winter explorers, and named it the most durable snowshoe in its guide. Its bindings are easy to adjust with gloves on, stay pliable in the cold and have toe stops that put your foot in the right spot every time. **Buying tip:** It comes in one 22-inch size rated to 180 pounds. GearJunkie notes that add-on 6-inch tails raise that to 250 pounds and help in deeper snow, so heavier hikers should buy the tails with it.
